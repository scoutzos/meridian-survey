Buildability due diligence

Questions to Ask the Agent Before We Buy Land

A practical call guide for confirming whether a parcel can legally, physically, and economically support the intended build.

Call objective

Get document-backed answers. Verbal confidence is helpful, but buildability should be confirmed through zoning, access, utilities, survey, septic/soil records, and county planning.

Buildability

What is the parcel ID/APN and exact acreage?
What is the current zoning?
Is a single-family home allowed by right, or would we need a variance, special use permit, or rezoning?
What are the setbacks, minimum lot size, road frontage, height limits, and lot coverage rules?
Are manufactured homes, modular homes, barndominiums, ADUs, or tiny homes allowed if relevant?
Has anyone tried to build here before, and were permits approved or denied?

Access

Does the property have legal recorded access, not just a visible path or driveway?
Is the road public or private?
If private, who maintains it and is there a recorded road-maintenance agreement?
Are there access easements across or to the property?
Will the driveway require a culvert, DOT permit, or shared-road approval?

Utilities

Is power at the property line? If not, how far away is the nearest pole or transformer?
Is public water and sewer available?
If not, has the land passed a perc test for septic?
Are there existing septic permits, well permits, soil reports, utility letters, or site plans?
What internet and cell service are available at the parcel?

Land Conditions

Is any part of the property in a flood zone, floodway, wetland, protected area, or conservation area?
Are there streams, drainage ditches, steep slopes, rock outcrops, or known soil issues?
Has the property been surveyed recently, and are the corners marked?
Are there old structures, buried tanks, dumping areas, environmental issues, or cleanup concerns?
The Meridian Collective Land Buildability Due Diligence
Agent Call Follow-Up Verify before offer hardens
Offer discipline: Make the offer contingent on confirming zoning, legal access, survey, septic/perc, utility availability, and builder review. The agent can help gather facts, but the county and recorded documents decide what can actually be built.

Documents to Request

Survey, deed, plat, parcel map, and recorded easements
Zoning confirmation, restrictions, HOA/covenants, and prior permit records
Perc/septic records, well records, utility letters, flood map, and soil report

Restrictions

Are there deed restrictions, covenants, HOA rules, architectural rules, or minimum home-size requirements?
Are mineral, timber, water, hunting, or agricultural rights included or reserved?
Are there leases, tenants, grazing rights, crop rights, or land-use agreements?
Is the parcel subject to conservation easements, rollback taxes, or current-use programs?

Money and Timeline

What are estimated tap fees, impact fees, permit fees, driveway/culvert costs, and utility-extension costs?
How long is the county taking to approve building, septic, driveway, and utility permits?
Will the seller allow a due-diligence period for zoning, survey, septic/perc, environmental, and builder review?
Are there known local contractors, surveyors, soil scientists, or builders who have worked nearby?

Red Flags to Slow Down For

No legal access A driveway or tire track is not enough. Confirm recorded access and maintenance duties.
No septic path Rural land without sewer needs soil and perc support before a home is realistic.
Floodway or wetlands Buildable acreage can shrink fast when regulated areas cross the lot.
Utility distance Power, water, and road improvements can quietly become five-figure costs.
Restrictive covenants Private restrictions can block otherwise legal build types.
Only verbal answers Ask for source documents and county confirmation before relying on claims.
